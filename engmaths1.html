Limits

A limit describes the value a function approaches as the input approaches some point — whether or not the function is actually defined there. It's the idea that makes the rest of calculus rigorous.

Formal idea

We write

$$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$$

to mean: as $x$ gets arbitrarily close to $c$ (from either side, without necessarily equalling $c$), $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to $L$. The precise $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition:

$$\text{For every } \varepsilon>0,\ \text{there exists } \delta>0 \text{ such that } 0<|x-c|<\delta \implies |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.$$

δ is how close x must be — ε is how close f(x) ends up.

A two-sided limit exists only if the left-hand limit $\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)$ and right-hand limit $\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)$ both exist and are equal.

Limit laws

RuleStatement
Sum$\lim [f(x)+g(x)] = \lim f(x) + \lim g(x)$
Product$\lim [f(x)\cdot g(x)] = \lim f(x)\cdot \lim g(x)$
Quotient$\lim \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \dfrac{\lim f(x)}{\lim g(x)}$, provided $\lim g(x)\neq 0$
Power$\lim [f(x)]^n = [\lim f(x)]^n$
SqueezeIf $g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)$ near $c$ and $\lim g=\lim h=L$, then $\lim f = L$

Indeterminate forms such as $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ don't tell you the limit directly — you must simplify (factor, rationalize) or apply L'Hôpital's rule ($\lim \frac{f}{g}=\lim\frac{f'}{g'}$) once derivatives are available.

Continuity

A function is continuous at $c$ when there's no gap, jump, or blow-up in its graph there — formally, when the limit at $c$ actually equals the function's value at $c$.

Definition

$f$ is continuous at $x=c$ if all three hold:

$$\text{1. } f(c) \text{ is defined} \qquad \text{2. } \lim_{x\to c} f(x) \text{ exists} \qquad \text{3. } \lim_{x\to c} f(x) = f(c)$$
TypeWhat failsLooks like
RemovableLimit exists, but $f(c)$ is undefined or wronga hole in the graph
JumpLeft and right limits exist but disagreethe graph steps up/down
Infinite$f(x)\to\pm\infty$ near $c$a vertical asymptote

Every differentiable function is continuous — but not every continuous function is differentiable (e.g. a sharp corner like |x|).

Differentiation

The derivative is a limit: the slope of the secant line through two points on a curve, as those points merge into one. That limiting slope is the instantaneous rate of change.

Definition

$$f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

Geometrically: $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ is the slope of the secant line through $(x, f(x))$ and $(x+h, f(x+h))$. As $h\to 0$, the secant rotates into the tangent line at $x$.

Differentiation rules

RuleFormula
Power$\dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$
Constant multiple$\dfrac{d}{dx}[k\,f(x)] = k\,f'(x)$
Sum/Difference$(f\pm g)' = f' \pm g'$
Product$(fg)' = f'g + fg'$
Quotient$\left(\dfrac{f}{g}\right)' = \dfrac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}$
Chain$\dfrac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$
Standard forms$\sin'x=\cos x,\ \ \cos'x=-\sin x,\ \ (e^x)'=e^x,\ \ (\ln x)'=\tfrac1x$

Partial Derivatives

When $f$ depends on more than one variable, you can still ask "how fast does it change?" — but now you must say in which direction. A partial derivative freezes every variable except one.

Definition

$$f_x(x,y) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h,y)-f(x,y)}{h} \qquad f_y(x,y) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x,y+h)-f(x,y)}{h}$$

Geometrically, slice the surface $z=f(x,y)$ with the plane $y=y_0$: the resulting curve's ordinary slope, in $x$, is $f_x(x_0,y_0)$. Slice with $x=x_0$ instead and you get $f_y(x_0,y_0)$. Every other variable is just held constant — ordinary single-variable rules apply to whichever one is "active".

Higher-order: $f_{xx}=\partial^2f/\partial x^2$, $f_{yy}=\partial^2f/\partial y^2$, and the mixed partial $f_{xy}=\partial^2f/\partial x\partial y$. For smooth functions, $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$ (Clairaut's theorem).

Gradient & Directional Derivatives

The gradient bundles both partials into one vector that points in the direction of steepest ascent. Rotate that idea and you can find the rate of change in any direction, not just along the axes.

Definitions

$$\nabla f(x,y) = \left\langle f_x(x,y),\ f_y(x,y) \right\rangle$$

For a unit vector $\mathbf{u}=\langle \cos\theta,\sin\theta\rangle$, the directional derivative of $f$ at $(x_0,y_0)$ in direction $\mathbf{u}$ is

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(x_0,y_0) = \nabla f(x_0,y_0)\cdot \mathbf{u} = f_x(x_0,y_0)\cos\theta + f_y(x_0,y_0)\sin\theta$$
FactMeaning
$\nabla f$ points toward steepest increaseLargest possible $D_{\mathbf u}f = |\nabla f|$, when $\mathbf u$ is parallel to $\nabla f$
$-\nabla f$ points toward steepest decreaseSmallest $D_{\mathbf u}f = -|\nabla f|$
$\nabla f \perp$ level curvesDirection of zero change is tangent to the contour through that point

Tangent Planes & Extrema

Just as a tangent line approximates a curve, a tangent plane approximates a surface near a point. And just as $f'(x)=0$ locates flat spots on a curve, $\nabla f = \mathbf 0$ locates flat spots on a surface — candidates for a max, min, or saddle.

Tangent plane & linear approximation

$$z = f(x_0,y_0) + f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0) + f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0)$$

This plane is the best flat approximation of the surface near $(x_0,y_0)$. Writing $dx=x-x_0,\ dy=y-y_0$, the linear approximation says

$$f(x_0+dx,\ y_0+dy) \approx f(x_0,y_0) + f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy$$

At a critical point where $f_x=f_y=0$, compute the discriminant

$$D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - (f_{xy})^2$$
ConditionConclusion
